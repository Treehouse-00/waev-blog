Above 6 dBi, conducted power must be reduced one-for-one in dB. Unlike 2400-2483.5 MHz, the 902-928 MHz band has no point-to-point relaxation of that trade. Every MeshCore node in North America transmits under a rule almost nobody has read. This is what it says.

<p><strong>TLDR:</strong> 47 CFR §15.247(a)(2) requires a measured 6 dB bandwidth of at least 500 kHz for any digitally modulated device in 902–928 MHz. This post works from a reference page compiled by **cisien** — *FCC Rules for 902–928 MHz and Where LoRa Chirp Spread Spectrum Fits*, at [stuff.cisien.com/fcc-900mhz-regulations.html](https://stuff.cisien.com/fcc-900mhz-regulations.html). That page assembled the citation list and, more usefully, found the FCC order that settles the "why 500 kHz" question rather than guessing at it. We have checked each citation against the current CFR text and the Commission's own orders, corrected two places where the arithmetic and the rationale had been run together, and added what the rule costs a MeshCore mesh in practice.

## The band is not free. It is conditioned.

902–928 MHz is unlicensed, which people hear as permissionless. It isn't. The FCC issues you no personal license, and in exchange sets hard conditions on how much power you emit and how wide you spread it — because the band is shared, on a noninterference basis, with government systems that were there first. The note appended to §15.247 is blunt about this: many of those are airborne radiolocation systems emitting high EIRP, and the Commission reserves the right to lower the Part 15 power limits if interference investigations warrant it.

The operating assumption behind every limit below is that anyone's transmitter can land on anyone else's frequency at any time, with no coordination. The rules are written to survive that.

## One number does the work

For a digitally modulated transmitter, §15.247(a)(2) is one sentence:

> Systems using digital modulation techniques may operate in the 902–928 MHz, 2400–2483.5 MHz, and 5725–5850 MHz bands. The minimum 6 dB bandwidth shall be at least 500 kHz.

The 6 dB bandwidth is the width of the signal measured between the points 6 dB down from its peak. For a single-channel LoRa radio — one chirp, one frequency — that measurement is essentially the entire compliance question. There is no processing-gain test to pass and no modulation category to fit into.

That last point matters more than it looks. The FCC has never classified chirp spread spectrum as direct sequence, as frequency hopping, or as anything else. It didn't have to: in 2002, the Second Report and Order in ET Docket 99-231 ([FCC 02-151](https://apps.fcc.gov/edocs_public/attachmatch/FCC-02-151A1.pdf)) generalized §15.247(a)(2) from *direct sequence spread spectrum* to *digital modulation* and removed the 10 dB processing-gain requirement. That is the only reason LoRa CSS has a path into this section at all. The rule became technology-neutral, and a bandwidth measurement became the whole test.

One practical warning the rule text hides: 500 kHz is a *measured* 6 dB bandwidth, not the nominal bandwidth field in your firmware. A radio configured for "500 kHz" can measure differently depending on spreading factor and implementation. Semtech's application note *AN1200.62, Best Practices for FCC Part 15.247 Compliance* is the vendor reference here. Measure it; don't assume it.

## Where 62.5 kHz actually lands

Read the rule straight and 62.5 kHz is outside §15.247. It is below the (a)(2) floor, and a single-channel LoRa transmission is not a hopping system, so §15.247(a)(1) doesn't take it either.

Outside §15.247 is not the same as prohibited. [§15.249](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-15.249) is a separate, current rule for the same band that imposes no bandwidth minimum whatsoever. It caps the fundamental at 50 mV/m field strength measured at 3 meters — about 0.75 mW EIRP — with harmonics at 500 µV/m.

This is a real door. It is also a very small room.

<Figure caption="Both doors on one axis. §15.247 buys headroom and charges 500 kHz for it. §15.249 asks nothing about bandwidth and gives back <b>roughly 24 dB</b> — a factor of about 270 in power.">

</Figure>

A typical MeshCore node running +20 dBm into a 3 dBi whip radiates about +23 dBm EIRP. The §15.249 ceiling is about −1 dBm EIRP. That is roughly 270× more power than the rule allows, and no amount of citing §15.249 changes it.

There is a second point the summaries usually skip. Part 15 authorization attaches to the **device**, under a **specific rule section**. A module certified under §15.247 does not inherit §15.249 by being reconfigured in firmware — it operates outside the terms of its own grant. The two sections are not interchangeable rooms you can walk between, and the FCC has said so on the record. In [DA 12-839](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DA-12-839A1.pdf), Starkey Laboratories asked the Commission to lower the §15.247(a)(2) minimum from 500 kHz to 100 kHz, and alternatively to waive it, so its narrowband assistive-listening and hearing-aid programming devices could use §15.247's power budget at 915 MHz. The Commission dismissed the petition and denied the waiver.

## Why 500 kHz — and why the obvious answer is wrong

The intuitive story is that 500 kHz is where the arithmetic works out. Total power is capped at 1 W no matter how wide you are, so spreading a watt across 500 kHz puts about 7.8 dBm in any 3 kHz slice — just under the 8 dBm/3 kHz power spectral density cap in §15.247(e). Anything narrower blows the cap. Tidy. Also not the reason.

The causality runs the other way. The 500 kHz minimum comes from the 1985 spread spectrum First Report and Order in Gen. Docket 81-413. The 8 dBm/3 kHz density cap was added five years later, in 1990, to ensure that energy is actually spread *uniformly* across that bandwidth — so a device can't nominally occupy 500 kHz while concentrating its power in a spike. The floor came first; the density cap polices how you fill it.

The Commission said what the floor is for in DA 12-839. The 500 kHz minimum was chosen to ensure that wideband digital devices operating with higher power than normally allowed under the Part 15 general emission limits would operate compatibly with other authorized radio systems — and specifically, in the Commission's words, to **exclude narrowband systems from using the higher power limits of Section 15.247**.

It is an eligibility rule. You can prove that from the boundary conditions, in both directions:

- A 62.5 kHz signal at +20 dBm sits around 7 dBm/3 kHz — *under* the density cap — and is still outside §15.247.
- A 500 kHz signal can *violate* the density cap if its spectrum is peaky rather than flat, and is still eligible under (a)(2).

Starkey made precisely the intuitive argument — that 100 kHz signals at the same 8 dBm/3 kHz density would carry less total power and therefore less interference potential — and the Commission rejected it. If you are going to cite a rationale for the floor, cite the eligibility purpose, not the arithmetic. The arithmetic is a coincidence that happens to land near the line.

## What this actually costs a MeshCore mesh

Here is the uncomfortable part, stated plainly. The community-coordinated North American setting — [910.525 MHz, 62.5 kHz, SF7, CR5](/blog/mesh-networking-at-a-hamfest/), the one this blog has cited in its own posts — is below the §15.247(a)(2) floor. So is Meshtastic LongFast at 250 kHz. The compliant alternative circulating in the MeshCore community, "MeshCore 500," is 902.250 MHz at 500 kHz, SF11, CR4:5.

The instinct is that going eight times wider must cost range. Run the numbers and it doesn't.

Thermal noise scales with bandwidth: 10 × log₁₀(500 / 62.5) = **9.0 dB** more noise in the wider channel. But [each spreading factor step buys about 2.5 dB](/blog/what-is-snr-lora/) of demodulation margin, and SF7 → SF11 is four steps — about **10 dB** back. Worked end to end, ignoring receiver noise figure, which is common to both:

| Preset | Noise in channel | Demod floor | Sensitivity |
|--------|------------------|-------------|-------------|
| SF7 @ 62.5 kHz | −126 dBm | −7.5 dB SNR | ≈ −133.5 dBm |
| SF11 @ 500 kHz | −117 dBm | −17.5 dB SNR | ≈ −134.5 dBm |

The compliant preset comes out about **1 dB more sensitive**. Range is not the price.

Airtime is. Symbol time is 2^SF / BW: 2.048 ms at SF7/62.5 kHz, 4.096 ms at SF11/500 kHz. Every packet takes roughly **twice as long on the air**, which means twice the channel occupancy, twice the collision window, and a mesh that saturates at half the traffic. On a [flood-routed network where one advert is heard and repeated by everyone in earshot](/blog/every-node-heard-it-twice/), that is the real constraint — not link budget.

That is the honest tradeoff, and it is an operator's decision, not ours. What a monitoring tool can do is tell you which one you're actually running, and what it costs you in [airtime and collisions](/blog/the-chatty-node/) once you've chosen.

## The rest of the rules that apply

| Requirement | Limit | Citation |
|-------------|-------|----------|
| Minimum 6 dB bandwidth | ≥ 500 kHz | §15.247(a)(2) |
| Peak conducted output power | 1 W | §15.247(b) |
| Alternative power measurement | Maximum Conducted Output Power, RMS-averaged across the signaling alphabet | §15.247(b)(3) |
| Antenna directional gain | 6 dBi, then reduce conducted power 1 dB per dB above — no point-to-point relaxation at 900 MHz | §15.247(b)(4) |
| Out-of-band attenuation | ≥ 20 dB in any 100 kHz outside the band; 30 dB if using RMS-averaged power | §15.247(d) |
| Power spectral density | ≤ 8 dBm in any 3 kHz band, continuous transmission | §15.247(e) |
| Restricted bands | Only spurious emissions; must also meet §15.209 field-strength limits | §15.205, §15.209 |
| RF exposure | MPE compliance; mobile/portable devices need a statement in the authorization application | §15.247(i), §1.1307, §1.1310, §2.1091, §2.1093 |
| User manual | Must warn that unapproved modifications void authority to operate | §15.21 |

The definitions of *spread spectrum*, *direct sequence*, and *frequency hopping* live in [§2.1](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-2.1), not Part 15 — the processing-gain-based definitions that used to sit in §15.3 are gone from the current text.
