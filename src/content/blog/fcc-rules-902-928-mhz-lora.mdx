<p><strong>TLDR:</strong> For US single-channel LoRa operation under FCC §15.247(a)(2), a signal must measure at least 500 kHz wide. A fixed 62.5 kHz channel falls below that minimum. Check your radio's approved settings before adopting a local mesh preset.</p>
</div>

A local mesh preset tells your radio how to reach its neighbors. The manufacturer's FCC documentation tells you which settings the radio is approved to use. Those two sets of settings need to agree.

That matters for anyone using a fixed 62.5 kHz channel in the US 902–928 MHz band, including the setup described in our [hamfest guide](/blog/mesh-networking-at-a-hamfest/). We should have made the bandwidth requirement clear alongside that preset.

## The width of your signal matters

Bandwidth is the amount of spectrum a signal occupies. Under [§15.247(a)(2)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-15.247), the minimum is **500 kHz**, measured at points 6 dB below the signal's peak. Transmit power has its own limits; turning it down does not satisfy the width requirement.

The FCC [explains the purpose](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DA-12-839A1.pdf): reserve this higher-power allowance for wideband systems sharing spectrum with other services. Frequency-hopping systems follow separate requirements. A LoRa chirp sweeping within one channel does not, by itself, qualify.

Narrower channels have other possible authorization paths. [Section 15.249](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-15.249) allows them at much lower emission levels—roughly equivalent to less than one milliwatt EIRP under free-space assumptions. EIRP accounts for both transmitter power and antenna gain. Changing firmware does not automatically qualify a radio under that rule.

## What happens to range?

A wider channel admits more noise. LoRa can compensate with a higher spreading factor, a setting that helps the receiver decode weaker signals but also changes transmission time.

For example, **SF7 at 62.5 kHz and SF11 at 500 kHz can offer similar receiver sensitivity** in a link-budget estimate. Comparable range is plausible; hardware and local interference still affect the result. Semtech's [design guide](https://meshtastic.org/assets/files/LoRa_Design_Guide-b3f1bb6c4d86b62a065c50d5961bc6b2.pdf) explains the tradeoff.

Allow for airtime too. Message length and radio settings determine how long each transmission occupies the channel. Repeaters add more transmissions, making [repeated traffic](/blog/the-chatty-node/) a cost the whole mesh shares.

## Check the radio, then plan the change

Use your model and FCC ID to find the manufacturer's approved settings, including antenna and power. If the documentation is unclear, ask which configurations the authorization covers. A “500 kHz” menu option alone cannot answer that question; compliance depends on measurements of the actual radio. Semtech's [FCC application note](https://www.mouser.com/pdfDocs/AN120062_BestPracticesforFCC_Rev_1_0_FINAL.pdf) covers those tests.

Agree on any change with your local group. A node switched to different settings may lose contact with its neighbors. Test familiar links together, send representative messages, and record which arrive. That gives the group a practical basis for its next decision.
