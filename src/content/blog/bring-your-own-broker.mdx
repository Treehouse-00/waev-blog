At some point, every mesh network operator running a shared infrastructure has the same conversation. Someone asks for MQTT access. You grant it. Another person asks, and you grant that too. A third person needs a narrower scope than the second — so now you're managing permissions, fielding access requests, and making judgment calls about what level of visibility each person should have. Access to the data has quietly become a bigger problem than collecting it.

Waev's answer to that problem isn't a broker you host. It's a credential that can only push, a read path that's always yours, and a stream that was never locked to one place to begin with.

## The access-list problem

A shared, centrally hosted server has an honest logic to it: one place to look, easy to set up. It works cleanly for a single operator monitoring their own gear. But a MeshCore community isn't a single operator. It's a ham club, a neighborhood emergency net, a CERT team. People join and leave. They want visibility into the parts they're responsible for, not everything. Managing that by hand turns into a half-finished permission matrix that nobody quite trusts.

Waev's ingest sidesteps the access-list problem at the credential level, not by asking you to run your own server. Each enrolled observer gets its own publish-only credential. That credential can send its own data and nothing else — it can't subscribe to another observer's topic, can't read the broker's state, can't alter or delete anything. There's no shared read access to manage, because there's no read access on that credential in the first place.

## What publish-only actually means

An enrolled observer publishes its packet stream directly to Waev's own MQTT ingest, at mqtt.waev.app. The credential it authenticates with is scoped to publishing: it can send the observer's own packets, and that's the extent of what it's allowed to do. It cannot subscribe to other topics, cannot read anything back over that connection, and cannot alter or delete anything on the broker — not its own data, not anyone else's.

That asymmetry is the whole design. An observer's job is to report what it heard. It never needed read access, administrative access, or visibility into any other observer's stream, so it doesn't have any of those things. The credential can do exactly one thing, which makes it easy to reason about: if it leaked, the worst it enables is more of the same data it already sends, not a read of your history or anyone else's.

## Reading your data back out

Publishing is only half the story — the data has to come back to be useful. The operator reads their data back out through a public read API. That's the supported way to get your own observer's history out of Waev's ingest for your own purposes, independent of the Live Map or Network Stats views Waev builds on top of the same stream.

## The stream isn't captive to one destination, either

The same observer stream can also be pointed at other tools of your choosing, using presets Waev ships in its own docs. That's the second half of "never-captive": it isn't only that you can read your data out of Waev — it's that the stream feeding Waev doesn't have to feed only Waev. An operator evaluating another analytics tool, or running one alongside Waev, isn't blocked by the credential model; the observer can feed more than one destination.

## What this means for your community

None of this is a slogan about data rights — it's a description of where the credential's permissions and the read path actually sit.

When someone in your group runs an observer, its credential is scoped to that observer alone. It doesn't inherit access to anyone else's stream, and nobody else's credential can read it. There's no shared server whose access list you have to maintain by hand.

When you want your own history, the public read API is there regardless of what Waev's own dashboards show you that day.

When you want to compare Waev against another tool, or feed a second tool for a different purpose, you can point the same observer stream at it using the presets in Waev's docs — you're not choosing one platform to the exclusion of every other.

That's the shape of "never-captive": a stream whose only way in is a credential that can't read anything back, and whose way out — to you, or to any other tool you choose — was never gated behind Waev alone.
