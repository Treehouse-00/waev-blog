The people on your mesh trusted you with something before they ever sent a packet. They assumed — maybe never consciously, but assumed — that the infrastructure wouldn't quietly profile them. No location estimates derived from hop paths. No named records accumulating in a database they didn't agree to. That trust is easy to violate and hard to reconstruct once broken.

This is a piece about data minimization. Not about values, but about mechanism — the specific technical choices that determine what Waev refuses to know, how that refusal is enforced, and where it doesn't reach.

<div class="callout">
<p><strong>TLDR:</strong> Waev's privacy model is three refusals in the ingest path: ADVERTs from opted-out nodes are dropped before any database write; companion devices' names and keys are blanked before analytics rows are written; and links that fail the physics gate stay off the default map. None of the three is absolute: raw frames, including companion ADVERTs, are archived and publicly retrievable, and companion device types and coordinates are kept in analytics rows.</p>

## The default is to collect

The easiest architecture for a network analytics platform is the greedy one: accept everything, store it all, figure out what to keep later. More data is usually better for building features. You can always delete things; you can't recover what you never collected.

That logic is tempting, and it's wrong.

What you store is what you're responsible for. A name field you never needed becomes a liability the moment a breach occurs. A device type you collected "just in case" is data someone can use to correlate activity across sessions. A hop-derived location estimate is a location record, even if you didn't call it that. The cost of data minimization is real — there are features you can't build without data you've refused to collect. We've accepted that cost. It's the right trade.

Data minimization isn't a privacy setting you can toggle on later. It's a decision you make in the data model, at ingest time, before anything is written. The choices we've made are concrete enough to describe field by field — including the fields that still get through. That's what this piece does.

## Gate 1: the opt-out check

The opt-out mechanism works by naming convention. Add ⛔, 🛑, or 🚫 to any MeshCore node name, and when an ADVERT carrying that name reaches Waev's ingest path, the name is checked before the packet is written to the database or to analytics.

It is not the first thing that happens to the packet. By the time the check runs, Waev has validated the observer's region code, mirrored the raw frame to the partner feed when a partner is connected, and decoded the packet. The check then drops the ADVERT ahead of the companion scrub, the topology pipeline, and every database write.

</Figure>

The reason ordering matters: if the opt-out check came after a database write, a partial record could exist for a node that asked not to be stored. Putting the check ahead of every database write closes that window for ADVERTs. It doesn't close it for packets that carry no name. A marked node's discovery response still creates a nameless node row, because the check reads the name and that packet has none. And a node that adds the marker after Waev has already mapped it keeps its earlier row and position until the 182-hour prune removes it.

Opt-out is not unconditional. An enrolled observer with a marker is not dropped; it becomes a private observer, hidden from the public map, topology, and network stats, although live packet frames still carry its device ID and region code. The drop itself is logged with the node's public key and name, and Workers Logs keeps a 5% sample of those events. The cleanup step that removes marked nodes clears their node, edge, identity, and score rows, but not their name history, their analytics rows, or their frames in the raw archive. The check is a runtime check in Waev's ingest code, so it is only as strong as that code.

One nuance worth naming: the opt-out is self-declared by the node's owner, by putting the marker in their node name. A network operator can't opt out another person's node on their behalf, and Waev can't opt out a node that hasn't been marked. The mechanism is decentralized on purpose. A person who controls their node name controls the marker.

## Gate 2: the companion problem

Personal MeshCore apps — the kind someone runs on a phone to send messages on a mesh network — broadcast on the same radio frequencies as repeaters and observers. That's by design: it's how a mesh works. Which means when your enrolled observer hears a relay packet, it may also hear ADVERTs from nearby companion devices. Those packets flow into the same MQTT stream.

We can't selectively receive RF. But we can be selective about what we write.

Companion devices are identified at ingest by the device type field in their ADVERT. When a companion ADVERT arrives from a device that isn't an enrolled observer, its name, public key, and source hash are blanked before Waev writes analytics rows or broadcasts the packet to the live feed, and the device gets no node-registry row and no map dot. What survives is more than the signal record: the device type, any GPS coordinates the ADVERT carries (kept in analytics rows for about 90 days), SNR, hop count, and timestamp. The full raw frame, with key, name, and position intact, goes to the packet archive, which the public `/packets/{hash}/raw` route serves.

</Figure>

The scrub covers ADVERTs, the one packet type that declares a device type. Other companion packets reach Waev unclassified. A direct message keeps its 1-byte source and destination address prefixes. A channel message carries no key, so its sender is never classified as a companion; on the Public channel, whose key is published, Waev decrypts the message to read the sender's display name, keeps the name, discards the text, and shows the name in Live Packets. A login request carries the sender's permanent public key in clear, and Waev stores that key in analytics and broadcasts it in the live feed.

The scrubbed analytics row has no name or key, but it is not anonymous in the sense that matters: it keeps the device type and any coordinates the ADVERT carried, and coordinates are a location record whether or not a name is attached. The archived raw frame still holds the key, name, and position.

The signal metadata does contribute to Network Stats — the aggregate counts and packet volumes. We're transparent about that: the RF traffic is real, and understanding it is useful for operators planning capacity.

Location deserves a direct answer. The latitude and longitude a companion puts in its own ADVERT are kept in analytics rows for about 90 days and in the raw archive. Separately, Waev infers positions for some nodes from path evidence, using a weighted centroid, and inference-placed nodes can appear on the map.

## The field-level view

The most concrete way to describe what we refuse is to look at the data model directly — which fields exist for each source type, and which don't.

<Figure caption="Data model by source type. <b>Operator nodes</b> carry full records, including topology edges labeled by evidence class. <b>Companion devices</b> have name and public key blanked; device type, ADVERT coordinates, and signal metadata are stored, and the raw frame is archived. <b>Opted-out ADVERTs</b> are not written to the database or analytics; a nameless node row can still come from the node's other packets.">

The differences are enforced by a check in the ingest code that runs on every packet, not by the shape of the database. The blanked fields exist in the data model; the check leaves them empty. That distinction matters: a field that doesn't exist can't be filled, but a check is code, and a code change can switch it off.

What Waev knows about a companion device on your mesh is what that figure shows: signal metadata, its device type, and any coordinates its ADVERTs carried, plus the raw frames in the archive.

## Why restraint is the honest position

There's a version of this piece that leans on trust language: "we respect your privacy," "your data is safe with us," that kind of thing. We've avoided that framing deliberately, not because we don't mean it, but because statements of intent aren't the interesting part.

The interesting part is: what would you find if you had full access to Waev's data? Today you would find no companion names or keys in the analytics rows. You would find companion device types and coordinates there, full raw ADVERT frames in the packet archive, and public keys from login requests. That answer is set by the ingest code and the data model, not by a privacy policy, which is why this post now describes the code as it runs.

We set out to collect the minimum that makes the product useful: signal quality for aggregate stats, evidence-labeled topology for the map, and full records from operator nodes that are deliberately running infrastructure. The raw-frame archive, the companion coordinates in analytics, and the login keys go beyond that minimum, and this post says so rather than describing them away.
