Your mesh doesn't know about <span class="waev-brand">Waev</span>. The nodes on your hilltop relays, the repeaters in the valley — they're broadcasting the same LoRa packets they always have, into the air, to whatever's listening. <span class="waev-brand">Waev</span> has no antenna. So the question, for anyone standing up their first observer, is a fair one: how does anything get from the radio to the browser tab?

<div class="callout">
<p><strong>TLDR:</strong> an enrolled observer listens on your mesh and publishes packets straight to Waev's own MQTT ingest, using a credential that can only publish — never read, alter, or delete anything on the broker. Waev drops opted-out ADVERTs and blanks companion names and keys at ingest, then builds the map with every line labeled by its evidence. The pipeline has five stages; each one does one job.</p>

## The observer: your network's window to the internet

A MeshCore observer is a repeater with an internet connection. On the radio side it operates exactly like any other repeater — forwarding packets through the mesh. On the internet side it has a path out, typically WiFi or Ethernet.

When a packet arrives at the observer's radio, the observer publishes a JSON record of that packet directly to Waev's MQTT ingest. That's the handoff — from radio to IP, from the air to the internet. Without an enrolled observer, <span class="waev-brand">Waev</span> sees nothing.

The word "enrolled" matters. <span class="waev-brand">Waev</span> draws topology only from observers you've connected — you gave the observer a publish-only credential scoped to your stream, and it publishes under that credential. An observer that isn't enrolled, or that publishes under someone else's credential, doesn't contribute to your map.

One observer is enough to start. Multiple observers give you overlapping vantage points — more measured hops, better coverage of nodes that sit far from any single connection point, and more independent evidence per edge. The map gets richer with each additional vantage point you add.

## Your stream: publish-only, never captive

The observer publishes to Waev's MQTT ingest (mqtt.waev.app) using a credential that is publish-only: it can send its own data and nothing else. It cannot subscribe to, read, alter, or delete anything on the broker — not its own stream, not anyone else's. There's no broker for you to host, patch, or keep online; that's Waev's infrastructure to run.

One subscriber does read the stream on Waev's side: when an affiliated partner is connected, Waev forwards observers' raw publishes for that partner's regions to a subscribe-only feed, before dedup and before the checks below.

You read your data back out through a public read API — the same one that feeds the Live Map, Live Packets, and the rest of the surfaces on waev.app.

The same observer stream can also be pointed at other tools of your choosing — Waev ships presets for that in its own docs, so nothing about the stream is captive to Waev. If you stop using Waev, the observer doesn't stop working; you point it somewhere else. [The Never-Captive Stream](/blog/bring-your-own-broker/) covers the credential model behind this in more depth.

## The ingest edge: where the picture gets checked

<span class="waev-brand">Waev</span> receives the stream from your observer and runs three checks, in order, before writing to its database.

**Opt-out markers drop the ADVERT.** If an ADVERT's node name contains a privacy marker — ⛔, 🛑, or 🚫 — and the node isn't an enrolled observer, the packet is discarded before any database or analytics write, and the drop is logged with the node's public key and name. Packets that carry no name are not checked.

**Companion ADVERTs lose their name and key.** MeshCore personal apps broadcast on the same RF mesh as your repeaters, so their packets reach your observers too. At ingest, a companion ADVERT's name and public key are blanked before analytics rows are written. The device type, any GPS coordinates, SNR, hop count, and timing are kept in analytics rows for about 90 days, and the raw frame is archived whole. The person doesn't appear on the map; the signal does, counted in Network Stats.

**Every map line carries its evidence class.** Lines measured by an enrolled observer are Proven or Corroborated; lines reconstructed from relayed path hashes are Inferred and drawn at reduced opacity; claimed links that fail the physics checks are Rejected and stay off the default map. Repeater ADVERT signatures are checked and recorded, but production runs that check in observe mode.

The result is a Live Map that labels what it measured, labels what it inferred, and stays quiet where it has nothing. A node no enrolled observer has heard won't appear — not because it doesn't exist, but because <span class="waev-brand">Waev</span> has no evidence of it.

## What each surface shows

Once packets clear the ingest edge, they're stored and routed to the relevant surfaces.

**Live Packets** is the stream as it arrives — privacy-filtered, timestamped, sorted by arrival. If a node is reaching Waev, it appears here first. It's the fastest way to check whether a given node is getting through.

**Live Map** draws topology edges labeled by evidence class, updated as new observations arrive. Edges don't disappear the moment a node goes quiet; they age. But the map won't draw a new edge to a silent node until fresh evidence arrives — see [why nodes go missing from the map](/blog/mesh-map-missing-nodes/) for how to tell that apart from a real dead zone.

**Network Stats** tracks activity over time: unique nodes active in the last hour, day, week. It uses both confirmed observer records and companion signal records with names and keys blanked, so the counts reflect real traffic — including devices that don't appear on the map.

**Packet Search** gives you a queryable history of non-private activity. Useful for after-action review, debugging a node that went quiet, or understanding traffic patterns over a specific time window.

**Outpost** is a per-node view: one node's activity timeline, heard-by list, and SNR history.

---

The five stages work together: the observer bridges RF to the internet, the stream reaches Waev without ever being captive to it, the ingest edge applies its checks, and the surfaces show what the network can prove about itself, labeled by how it knows. When something looks off on the map — a missing node, a gap in topology — tracing back through those five stages is usually enough to find where the observation chain broke.
