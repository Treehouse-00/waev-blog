If you're running a mesh network for a community that depends on it, you already know what it actually takes. Quiet work, unglamorous, and genuinely important.

<span class="waev-brand">Waev</span> is a real-time analytics platform for MeshCore networks. It exists because people like you needed a way to understand what those networks are actually doing — live packets, signal per hop, coverage, the health of the infrastructure you keep running, often alone, often without much tooling. And it’s built around a simple idea: your community’s data belongs to you, not us.

## 01 · Privacy is the default, not a dial

The conversations on your mesh are real. The people having them trust the infrastructure you built. We don't take that lightly.

Two rules do most of the work. First, **opt-out**: add a privacy marker — ⛔ 🛑 🚫 — to any node name and Waev drops that node's ADVERTs before they are stored, mapped, or counted. Second, **companion devices are scrubbed before analytics rows are written**. MeshCore personal apps broadcast on the same radio mesh as your repeaters, so those packets reach our observers. At ingest, we blank the name and public key of a companion ADVERT and give the device no place in the node registry or on the map.

*Correction, November 9, 2026: this section used to say opt-out is absolute and that only anonymous signal metadata survives the companion scrub. Neither is true today. A connected partner feed receives every raw frame before the opt-out check; the check covers ADVERTs, not packets without a name, and logs the node's public key and name when it fires; records written before a marker was added are not all removed. The companion scrub keeps the device type and any GPS coordinates in analytics rows for about 90 days; the raw-frame archive keeps the full ADVERT and serves it publicly; Public-channel messages show the sender's display name; and login requests carry the sender's permanent public key, which Waev stores. The value card above is the standard; this note is where the system stands against it.*

If you're running a companion app, you won't appear on the <span class="waev-brand">Waev</span> map or in its node registry. Your ADVERTs still reach the raw-frame archive and analytics rows as the correction above describes, and a message you post on the Public channel shows your display name in Live Packets. An operator who enrolls a companion as an observer chooses to connect it; a privacy marker in that observer's name makes it a private observer, hidden from the public map, topology, and network stats, while live packet frames still carry its device ID and region code.

Privacy here isn't a setting. It's the floor. [What we choose not to know](/blog/what-we-choose-not-to-know/) goes deeper into why that's the right default.

## 02 · If we can’t verify it, you won’t see it

When you look at your network map, everything on it should be there because something verifiable put it there. Not because we guessed.

Every edge in the topology carries an evidence class. **Proven** and **Corroborated** lines trace back to receptions an enrolled observer measured. **Inferred** lines are reconstructed from relayed path hashes — 1-byte to 3-byte key prefixes that nobody signs — and the default map draws them at reduced opacity. **Rejected** links fail the physics gate and stay off the default map. Repeater ADVERT signatures are checked and recorded, but production runs the check in observe mode, so an invalid signature does not stop an advert.

*Correction, November 9, 2026: this section used to say every edge traces back to an enrolled observer or an authenticated repeater, and that spoofed or inferred prefixes are rejected. The default map draws Inferred lines, and signature checks do not block adverts.*

## 03 · Hard to read is a bug

You've probably used dashboards that made the network seem more confusing than it actually is. Charts that don't explain themselves. Colors that mean nothing. Density for density's sake. We have too.

We think a dashboard that's hard to read is wrong, not just ugly. Clarity is part of correctness. Color should mean something. Motion should explain, not decorate. If a screen makes a complex thing feel more complex, it's not done yet.

That's a constraint from the first sketch, not polish at the end. The practical upshot: when something is actually wrong on your network, you should see it — not hunt for it.

## 04 · Speed is a promise

Packets should appear as they arrive. A map should pan without lag. A query that covers your whole mesh should come back before you wonder if it's stuck.

There's a real difference between a tool you have to check and a tool you can actually watch. That's what we're building.

## 05 · Your community's data stays yours

You built your mesh. You recruited the operators, configured the nodes, solved the RF problems, and kept it running. That community — and its data — belongs to you.

The old model, where everyone requests access to a central set of MQTT servers, turns into a management problem fast: scoped here, full access there, a half-dozen requests a day, every one a judgment call. We've watched it happen. We'd rather invert it entirely.

**Your observers publish on credentials that can never read. The same devices can feed other tools — the presets are in our own docs — and the read API is public. Nothing locks your stream to Waev.**

One more consumer sits on Waev's side of that stream: when an affiliated partner is connected, Waev forwards observers' raw publishes for that partner's regions to a subscribe-only feed, before dedup and before Waev's own privacy filters.

You keep control of where your observers publish. We get what we need to build useful tools. Nobody manages an access list. It's a better arrangement for everyone — and it happens to be the honest one.

## 06 · Built deliberately, not on demand

We use the word “opinionated” as a description, not a warning. Principled about what we build. Honest about what we won’t.

We try to build in an order that makes sense for the whole tool, not just whatever came up most recently. That means we might not build what you ask for, or we might get to it later than you'd like.

Bug reports we take seriously — always. Feature suggestions we read carefully. Sometimes we build them, sometimes we don't, and if it's the latter we'll say so.

That's the deal.

Mesh networks don't maintain themselves. The people who run them — who tune the RF, place the repeaters, recruit the members, keep it alive through outages and upgrades and all the invisible work in between — that's who this is for.

These six things are how we stay honest about what we're building, and why.
